# Layer 2: Mapping (class notes)

**What this layer is, in one line:** it turns a radar picture into a map showing, for every small square of ground, how likely it is to be a flooded rice field.

These notes explain the ideas. You do not need to know how the code is written.

#%% md
## 1. The goal

Layer 1 gave us radar pictures where flooded ground looks dark, and hand-marked examples. Layer 2 turns each picture into a map. For every **20 m by 20 m square (a pixel)** the map holds a number from 0 to 1: **the probability that this square is a flooded rice field.**

The key requirement is that the number must be **honest**. If the map says 0.7 for a hundred squares, about 70 of them should really be flooded. Honest numbers are called **calibrated**. This matters because Layer 3 uses these numbers to say how sure it is.

Remember two radar words from Layer 1: **VV** and **VH** are two readings the satellite takes of the same ground (VV is the best for spotting water, VH responds to vegetation), measured in **dB**, where lower means darker. Water is dark.

#%% md
## 2. The recipe, as a picture

![Layer 2 pipeline: radar chip, baseline and two predictors, fusion, calibration, paddy mask, final map](attachment:s2_pipeline.png)

Read it top to bottom. In words:

1. Start with the radar chip.
2. A **baseline** (a very simple guess) is run on the side. We keep it only so we can show our real methods do better.
3. **Two predictors** each make their own guess.
4. **Fusion** blends their two guesses into one number.
5. **Calibration** checks the number is honest and fixes it if not.
6. **Paddy mask**: keep only rice-field squares.
7. The result is `p_flooded_paddy`.

Each step is explained next.

#%% md
## 3. The baseline: Otsu's method

**Otsu's method** (named after its inventor, Nobuyuki Otsu; pronounced "oh-tsoo", nothing to do with the game osu) is a classic way to pick **one brightness cut-off** for a picture. Imagine the brightness of all pixels drawn as a histogram. If a picture has water and land, you usually see two humps, a dark one (water) and a bright one (land). Otsu's method picks the cut-off that best separates the two humps. Pixels darker than the cut-off are called water, the rest are called land.

It is quick and a standard thing to try, which is why it is our **baseline**: the plain method we must beat.

Its weaknesses:

* It answers only **yes or no**, never "maybe", so there is no probability.
* It assumes every picture has two clear humps. A picture with almost no flood has only one hump, and Otsu still draws a cut-off through it, wrongly calling lots of land "water".

On the test events it gives a calibration error of **0.338** (very dishonest numbers) and an overlap score of only **0.190** (explained in section 7).

#%% md
## 4. Predictor 1: the rule stack

This predictor is a set of hand-written rules, like a checklist an expert would use. The main one: pick a brightness threshold for each picture, and call pixels darker than it water. Several safeguards stop it going wrong, for example using sensible default thresholds when a picture does not clearly separate into two humps, and working in small blocks of the picture so one dark corner does not distort the rest.

Instead of a hard yes or no, it produces a **soft score** between 0 and 1: the further below the threshold a pixel is, the closer to 1.

An honest note: on the validation events this rule-based predictor actually did better than our final blend on one score (overlap 0.491 against 0.356), so we report that openly.

#%% md
## 5. Predictor 2: the boosted pixel model

This is a **machine-learning** model. It learns from the hand-marked chips which pixel patterns mean water. "Boosted" means it is built from many small **decision trees**, each a tiny set of yes/no questions. Trees are added one by one and each new tree is trained to correct the mistakes of the ones before. Together they vote, giving a probability.

It looks at features of each pixel such as the VV and VH values, how bright the neighbours are, and how bumpy the neighbourhood is. It is trained only on the **train** events.

#%% md
## 6. Fusion and calibration

**Fusion.** The two predictors make different mistakes, so we blend them. The blend uses **logistic regression**, a standard method that combines numbers into a probability. We use separate blending settings for each kind of land (trees, crops, towns, and so on), because a predictor may be more trustworthy on one kind than another. The blend is fitted on the **validation** events, which neither predictor was trained on, so the weights are fair.

**Calibration.** After blending, we check whether the numbers are honest. We tried three fixes: leave the numbers alone ("identity"), stretch or squash them with one number ("temperature"), or reshape them with a flexible curve ("isotonic"). We chose by testing on validation events. **Leaving them alone won**, meaning the blend was already honest enough.

**How we measure honesty: ECE.** Sort pixels into bands by their stated probability. In each band compare the stated probability with how often pixels really were flooded. ECE (expected calibration error) is the average gap. **Smaller is better**, and 0 would be perfectly honest.

#%% md
## 7. Scores in plain words

* **ECE**: how dishonest the probabilities are (above). Lower is better.
* **Brier score**: the average squared gap between the probability and the truth (0 or 1). Lower is better.
* **AUC**: how well the map ranks flooded squares above dry ones. 1 is perfect, 0.5 is guessing.
* **IoU (overlap score)**: draw the true flooded area and the predicted flooded area. IoU is the area where both agree divided by the area covered by either. 1 is perfect, 0 means no overlap.

#%% md
## 8. The paddy mask, and an honest limit

The insurance question is about **rice fields**, but the land-cover map we use (ESA WorldCover) has no rice class. So we use **cropland** as a stand-in. This overcounts a little, because cropland includes crops other than rice. And the hand labels mark **water**, not "flooded rice", so some of what we score is flooded land that is not rice. We say this whenever we quote results.

#%% md
## 9. Results on test events (events not used for fitting)

| Measure | Our final map | Otsu baseline |
|---|---|---|
| ECE (honesty, lower is better) | **0.0387** | 0.338 |
| IoU (overlap, higher is better) | **0.419** | 0.190 |
| AUC | **0.934** | not applicable (yes or no only) |
| Brier | **0.0627** | not reported |

Flooded area inside the paddy mask on the test events: the hand labels say **8.56 km2**, our calibrated map says **5.11 km2** (it under-counts), and Otsu says **16.01 km2** (it over-counts badly).

Two honest points. Inside the paddy mask alone, calibration is worse (ECE 0.0992), so the map is less trustworthy there than overall. And on validation the simple rule predictor scored better overlap than our final blend, as mentioned.

#%% md
## 10. Duration of flooding (partly simulated)

Crops survive short floods and die in long ones, so insurance cares about **how many days** a field stayed under water. A radar chip is a single snapshot, so we cannot see duration directly. We build a **semi-synthetic** duration map. That means partly simulated, and we always label it so.

* We know from Layer 1 that the satellite passes only every few days, so a flood's end is known only as a range between two passes. The map estimates the chance a field stayed flooded at least d days, for d = 3, 7, 10 and 14, using a standard method for range-type data (the **Turnbull** estimator).
* To turn duration into crop damage we use **survival curves** for rice: intolerant rice survives about 7 days of full submergence (half die), flood-tolerant "SUB1" varieties about 14 days. **These curves are assumptions, not fitted to data.** We say so.
* On the test events the duration step shows a sensitivity of 0.468 and a specificity of 0.979.

#%% md
## 12. Glossary

* **Baseline**: a simple method we must beat.
* **Calibrated**: probabilities that match reality (70 percent means about 70 percent).
* **Decision tree**: a tiny set of yes/no questions that ends in a guess.
* **ECE**: average gap between stated probability and actual frequency.
* **Fusion**: blending several predictors into one.
* **IoU**: overlap between predicted and true flooded area.
* **Pixel**: one 20 m by 20 m square.
* **Semi-synthetic**: partly simulated, and labelled so.
* **VV, VH**: two radar readings; water is dark in VV.

## 13. Review questions

1. **What does Layer 2 do?** Turns a radar picture into a calibrated probability, per pixel, of flooded rice field.
2. **What is Otsu's method and why is it here?** A classic way to choose one brightness cut-off. It is our baseline.
3. **Why two predictors?** They make different mistakes, so blending helps.
4. **What does "boosted" mean?** Many small decision trees, each correcting the previous ones.
5. **What is calibration?** Making stated probabilities match real frequencies.
6. **Which calibration won?** None needed; leaving it unchanged was best.
7. **What is ECE?** The average gap between stated probability and actual frequency. Lower is better.
8. **How much better than the baseline?** On test events honesty improved from 0.338 to 0.0387 and overlap from 0.190 to 0.419.
9. **What is a weakness?** The paddy mask is a stand-in (cropland), the labels are water, and on validation a simple rule predictor beat the blend on overlap.
10. **Is the duration part real?** No. It is semi-synthetic and the crop survival curves are assumptions.
